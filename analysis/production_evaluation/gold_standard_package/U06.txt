[T001] Moderator: That distinction John just drew — busy versus not bothering — feels like it lands somewhere real. I want to move us on to something connected. You've all been talking about food choices as if they're pretty personal, practical, individual decisions. I'm curious whether being a man shapes any of that — whether gender comes into it at all, in how you decide what to eat, or what feels acceptable to eat. What do you all make of that?
[T002] Participant 1: I think it does, yeah. I mean, not in a way I'm thinking about constantly, but when you step back... there's definitely something there.

Like, I eat meat most days — few times a week — and I don't think twice about it. But I notice when women I know talk about food, there's sometimes this guilt thing attached, isn't there? Calories, what they "should" be eating, whether something's indulgent. And then there's the opposite — I've got mates who won't eat salad without making a joke about it, you know? Like they have to announce it's not a real meal or something. Which is weird when you think about it.

For me personally, meat's just... it's part of how I eat. Not something I've decided on, it's just what I do. And I don't feel bad about it or defensive about it. I eat chicken, I eat beef, fish sometimes. It's normal to me. But I'm aware that's probably because nobody's ever made me feel like I should justify it or feel awkward about it. Whereas I think if you're a woman choosing to eat meat, there might be more... I don't know, commentary? And then if you're a bloke choosing not to, there's this whole other thing that gets attached to it, isn't there?

I've never really thought about going vegetarian or anything like that — it doesn't appeal to me — but I wouldn't judge someone for doing it. The fact that people do seem to judge, especially if it's a man... that feels off to me, if I'm being honest. Like, what's that about?
[T003] Moderator: Andrew's picked up on something interesting there — this idea that being a bloke who doesn't eat meat brings a different kind of social weight to it. Does that land for anyone else? Or does it feel different from your experience?
[T004] Participant 2: Yeah, I'd say that's real. I mean, I've definitely seen it — bloke says he's vegetarian or he doesn't eat red meat, and there's this... I don't know, this immediate thing that happens. Someone'll make a comment, or there's this bit of piss-taking that goes beyond just banter, you know? It's got a different edge to it.

I think part of it is the whole idea that meat-eating gets tied up with being a man in this weird way. Like it's supposed to be normal and unquestioned, and if you're not doing it, there's this assumption that something's... off. Which is daft when you actually think about it. But it's there.

I've got friends who've gone vegetarian, and I've watched the reaction from other blokes — it's not just gentle ribbing, it's like they're making some kind of statement, you know? Even if they're just saying they don't fancy meat anymore. The lads treat it like they've made some weird choice that needs explaining or defending.

Whereas for me, eating meat — nobody cares. Nobody needs to know why, it's just what I do. It's the default, so it doesn't need justifying. But flip it around and suddenly it does. That's the thing that gets me — it's not actually about the food, it's about this weird masculine thing that's got attached to it, and I think that's... I mean, that's just silly, isn't it? If someone doesn't want to eat meat, that's their choice. It shouldn't be a thing.
[T005] Participant 1: Yeah, and I think what's underneath it — what I've noticed anyway — is that it's not really about the food at all. It's about control, isn't it? Or proving something. Like, if a bloke says he's not eating meat, some lads take it as a challenge to their... I don't know, their territory or something. 

I've got a mate, been vegetarian for maybe five years now, and the first time he said it there was this whole thing — jokes about him being weak, needing his protein, all that. But here's the thing: he's a bigger bloke than most of us, plays rugby, genuinely doesn't care what anyone thinks. And over time the piss-taking just... died away, because he wasn't bothered by it. He wasn't defending himself or getting wound up about it. It was just what he ate.

But I think what gets the reaction in the first place is that when someone steps outside what's expected — what feels normal to everyone else — there's this instinct to push back. To reassert the thing that's supposed to be normal. And yeah, that's tied up with masculinity somehow, but it's more about the group, I reckon. It's like, "we do this, that's who we are, and if you're not doing it you're saying something about us."

Which is daft, like Nick says. But it's real. And I think that's why I wouldn't bat an eye at a mate going vegetarian now — I'm old enough not to care about that sort of thing. But I get why younger blokes might give someone grief over it. Doesn't make it right, but I understand where it's coming from.
[T006] Participant 2: Yeah, that's exactly it. Your mate playing rugby — that's the thing right there. He's not bothered defending it, so there's nothing to push against. The piss-taking only works if it lands, doesn't it?

I think that's the bit that actually bothers me more, if I'm honest. It's not the fact that blokes eat meat or don't eat meat — that's neither here nor there. It's this idea that you have to defend it, or that there's some kind of... test you have to pass to prove you're still one of the lads or whatever. That feels immature, you know? Like, we're not kids anymore.

And I've seen it go the other way too, up here especially. There's this thing where if you're not eating a full cooked breakfast or you're having a salad, you get comments. Not aggressive necessarily, but it's there — this assumption that you're doing something a bit soft. And it's just food, isn't it? It doesn't say anything about who you are.

The thing Andrew said about territory — I reckon that's spot on. It's not really about the vegetarianism itself, it's about someone doing something different and the group going, "No, this is how we do things." Which is... I mean, that's just insecurity dressed up as banter, really. And once you're past that — once you're forty, you've got your own life sorted, you don't need to prove anything — you just don't care. You eat what you want and nobody makes it weird. Except some people still do, which is the odd bit.
